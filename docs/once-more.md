# Once More: a game about morality with no morality meter

Ten situations out of Dostoevsky and Nietzsche, and no alignment bar — because
the bar is the thing being argued about. What the game has instead is a deck.

---

## One · The deck is the argument

You start with twelve cards and no character. Every choice spends one and puts
back whatever the doing of it teaches, so after ten scenes the deck is a record
of what you repeatedly did.

That is the *Genealogy of Morals* as a rule rather than as a summary. A morality
is not a set of principles you consult; it is the **residue of a history**. And
because a choice is only offered if the card it costs is in your hand, the deck
is simultaneously the list of what you are still *able* to do.

Play mercy for twenty years and cruelty stops being available — not because the
game forbids it, but because it is not in the deck any more. Play cunning for
twenty years and you will find, at the scene where somebody needs you on a
Tuesday, that sacrifice is not something you have. The doors close as they open.
Both men agree about this completely; they part over whether to be pleased.

### Guilt is the card that cannot be played

It is the one card with no use. It occupies a slot in a hand of five and does
nothing, which means a guilty person is a person with fewer options — as a
mechanic, not as an image.

The only thing that removes it is **Confession**, which clears every Guilt in
the deck and costs all the standing those acts bought, plus one, so that it is
never merely a good trade.

That single rule is *Crime and Punishment* compressed — Raskolnikov's problem is
never that the arithmetic was wrong, it is that he cannot go back into a room
with his mother in it — and it is *at the same time* Nietzsche's second essay,
where *Schuld* (guilt) is shown descending from *Schulden* (debts) and morality
turns out to be bookkeeping that has forgotten it was bookkeeping.

The two of them are describing the same machine. They disagree about whether to
be grateful for it. Having them both comment on the same rule is the shortest
way to show that.

## Two · Both voices, and why neither is a quotation

Nothing either man says here is presented as a quotation. Both voices are **this
game's own rendering of the argument**, written from the passage named under
each scene.

That is partly a rights matter and partly a better teaching decision.

The rights half: both are long out of copyright in the original, and neither is
in English. Every English Dostoevsky is a translator's own copyrighted work, and
so is the readable Nietzsche. The Book Stall elsewhere in this arcade already
handles that carefully, naming a translator on every receipt.

The teaching half is the more interesting one. **A paraphrase is something you
can argue with. A quotation is something you can only admire.** A line in
quotation marks arrives with its authority attached and closes the question; the
same argument in the game's own words invites you to check it against the
chapter cited underneath, which is what a person learning this material should
be doing anyway.

A handful of terms that have entered ordinary English — the will to power, the
last man, amor fati, ressentiment, the eternal recurrence — appear as terms
rather than as quotes.

### They are not a scoreboard

Neither voice is ever simply pleased with you. They are not opposites with the
correct answer sitting between them, and they agree about the diagnosis far more
often than anyone expects — Nietzsche called Dostoevsky the only psychologist he
had anything to learn from (*Twilight of the Idols*, "Skirmishes" 45), and it
shows.

Where they both object to the same choice, that is the choice worth thinking
about. That happens most often on the free option, which is nearly always the
passive one.

---

## Three · The free choice

Every scene has exactly one option that costs no card, so a life can never
dead-end. It is checked at runtime rather than trusted — `audit()` runs on mount
and shows an alarm if any scene lacks one, because the failure mode is silent
and would only hit the unlucky.

But the free option is almost always the one where you do not act: walk past the
door, carry the insult, agree in the room and disagree in your head, promise and
mean it entirely and go for five weeks. Doing nothing is free at the point of
use and it is not free.

That is the single most Dostoevskyan thing in the design, and Nietzsche's
objection to it is different from his and every bit as hard.

---

## Four · The ending is not a score

After ten scenes the game lays your whole life out as a numbered list and asks
the question from *The Gay Science* 341: this, in the same order, innumerable
times more, with nothing new in it. **Do you want it again?**

Nothing hangs on the answer except the only thing that could. Say yes and the
reading is measured against affirmation; say no and you are asked which one you
would take back, which is the only question in the game with nothing riding on
it and therefore the only one that finds out what you actually think.

The reading itself is matched against the deck you finished with, not a tally of
good deeds — ten positions, from The Extraordinary Man to Amor Fati by way of
The Last Man and The Grand Inquisitor. None of them is a win. The two everybody
aims at both arrive with a bill.

## Five · Things the first playtest caught

- **The opening deck was four kinds.** Scene one dealt the Grand Inquisitor and
  locked two of its three answers, which is a fine sentence and a bad game.
  Nobody begins a life with no faith and no arithmetic. It opens with twelve
  cards now — two each of the six ordinary ones — because the interesting thing
  is not what gets added, it is what gets pruned.
- **Two scenes had no free choice.** Both would have dead-ended a life for
  anyone holding the wrong hand. That is why the audit exists.
- **The faces are drawn, not photographed.** By Picture Book's own hand, in the
  same crayon, with the same torn ground. A photograph of a real man beside a
  paraphrase of his argument implies the paraphrase is a quotation. A drawing
  does not make that claim — and three or four marks (a high pale forehead and a
  long straggling beard; a moustache doing most of the work of the silhouette)
  are enough to be recognised by anyone who knows the face and not to mislead
  anyone who does not.
