# Name and Form: two thousand out of the epics, in one hand

*Nāma-rūpa* — name and form. Two thousand figures out of the Mahābhārata, the
Rāmāyaṇa and the asura literature, drawn by the same hand as Picture Book.

The same hand is not a figure of speech. The head, the ears, the crowded
features, the torn ground, the pencil scuff over every fill and the grain on top
are all *imported* from `book/portrait.ts` rather than copied. That is the point
of building it this way.

Picture Book makes one claim: that a generator can hold a single illustrator's
hand steady across a whole cast. A hundred neighbours is a weak test of that —
they are all the same kind of subject. The honest test is to hand the same hand
a completely different one, with crowns and matted locks and fangs and serpent
hoods in it, and see whether it still looks like one book. Everything new in
this effect is **iconography**, not style.

---

## One · What a card is, and what it is not

There is no likeness in any of this and there could not be. These are not people
who were photographed; they are people who were *described*.

So the roster records only what the texts record. Each named figure carries a
short list of tags — that this one is dark as a rain cloud, that one wears
matted locks piled and tied, that one has fangs, that one bound her own eyes on
her wedding day and never unbound them — and the generator draws a face under
it. Anything the texts do not specify comes from the seed.

That division is the whole reason the gallery can be trusted. Rama is blue and
crowned and marked with the ūrdhva puṇḍra because those are given. His eyebrows
are whatever the seed says, because nothing is given about his eyebrows.

**268 of the two thousand are figures the texts name.** The rest are the host.

---

## Two · The host, and why it is honest

The epics are full of thousands who are counted and never named. Eighteen
akshauhinis at Kurukshetra. The vanara army at Kishkindha and the five days of
carrying stones to the water. Ravana's night-rangers; the fourteen thousand at
Janasthana; the Kālakeyas who hid in the sea by day; the gaṇas on the mountain;
the twelve Ādityas; the seven ashrams.

Those are generated. Names are compounded the way the real ones are — a
qualifying first element and a noun, *Ugra* + *senā*, *Chitra* + *ketu*, *Vīra* +
*bāhu* — and some of what comes out collides with names in the texts, which is
expected, because the epics are drawing from the same box of morphemes.

Every one of those cards says on its face which host it belongs to and that the
verse does not name it. That seemed better than either padding the roster with
invented "characters" or pretending the epics contain only the people with
speaking parts.

## Three · The grammar is not a matter of taste

A face in this style is a face. What makes it Ravana rather than a greengrocer
is a very short list of marks, and the list is a thousand years old:

| mark | means |
| --- | --- |
| kirīṭa mukuṭa — a tall tapering cone | sovereignty |
| karaṇḍa mukuṭa — shallow stacked tiers | rank below it |
| jaṭā — matted locks piled and tied | renunciation |
| ūrdhva puṇḍra — a vertical U | Vaishnava |
| tripuṇḍra — three horizontal lines | Shaiva |
| a third eye, drawn vertically | Shiva |
| a fan of hoods behind the shoulders | nāga |
| tusks below the lip | rākṣasa |
| a prabhāmaṇḍala behind the head | divinity |

None of it is invented here. It is legible precisely because it is fixed.

Complexion is in the same category and is worth saying out loud: in this
material skin colour is not a variable, it is **information**. Rama and Krishna
are described as dark as a rain cloud and have been painted blue for it for
centuries. Shiva is ash from the cremation ground. A rākṣasa on a manuscript
page is green or smoke. So complexions are set by tag, never by seed, for anyone
the texts describe.

## Four · Things the screenshots caught

- **Everyone was a yellow poncho.** Armour was filled in the crown's gold, and
  the eye reads a large flat area of the same hue as the crown as one garment. A
  kavacha is a *breastplate strapped over a body*: bare shoulders first, then a
  plate in its own metal, with bands, a boss and two outlined shoulder guards.
  Helmets moved to the same metal for the same reason — a thousand soldiers in
  gold helmets is a thousand identical soldiers.
- **The nāga hood was a jester's hat.** Drawn as a disc on a stalk, which is the
  obvious way. A cobra hood is wider than it is tall and flares at the *top*.
- **The turban plume was smoke.** A thin white stroke coming off the top of a
  turban always is. It had to be a filled leaf with a spine down it.
- **Four faces were earmuffs.** Brahma's extra heads sat at ear height in a
  washed-out tint. They are now at brow height, in the figure's own complexion,
  and they get eyes.
- **Jatayu was a man.** He is a vulture in the poem, and Ganesha is
  elephant-headed, and there is no honest way to draw either as a human face
  wearing a hat. Both got their own head: a hooked beak with a feather ruff, and
  fan ears with a trunk and one broken tusk.

## Five · What it costs

Two thousand figures at 132 pixels a cell, thirteen to a side, is twelve plates.
A figure here is two to three thousand canvas strokes — a crown is a dozen
shapes, a beard several hundred strands, a nāga's hood seven of everything — so
the plates are handed to the globe one at a time as they finish. The first
hundred and sixty-nine go up while the rest are still being drawn, which turns a
blank wait into a gallery filling up.

The filter tabs re-generate rather than re-index: choosing *Rāmāyaṇa* or *the
named* builds and bakes only that set, which is quick, and the named-only view
(268 cards) is the one worth opening first.

## A note on the subject

These are living figures for a great many people, and the register this is drawn
in — warm, plain, picture-book — is the register the material has been given in
India for as long as there have been children's editions of it. That is the
tradition being worked in, not a joke at its expense. Nothing here claims to be
a likeness, no card asserts anything the texts do not, and the two thousandth
card says as plainly as the first that the verse counted this person and did not
name them.
