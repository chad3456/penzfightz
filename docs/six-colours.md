# Six Colours: two thousand drawings, six inks each

## The rule is printed on the drawing

The reference pictures this study is drawn from carry their own palette in the
corner — a stack of six rounded pills — and every line in the picture is one of
them. That is not a flourish. It is **the rule the drawing was made under,
printed on the drawing**, and it turns out to be the entire discipline of the
style.

With six inks and no mixing you cannot render anything. You cannot shade, you
cannot blend, you cannot correct, you cannot even reach for the colour the thing
actually is. What you can do is *choose*, over and over, and the choosing is
what the picture is made of.

So every drawing here draws six from a pool of eighteen and may use nothing else
for its line work. The strip in the corner is generated from the same six, which
makes it a claim you can check rather than a decoration.

## The colour is wrong on purpose

This is the thing to understand about the style, and it is easy to state and
hard to hold your nerve on: **the colour of the marks has nothing to do with the
colour of the object**. A cola bottle comes out in six colours that may contain
no red at all. A teapot gets the palette a fire extinguisher would want.

There is exactly one place local colour survives — a layer of broad translucent
fills that goes down *first* — and everything after it is free. That ordering is
the whole architecture:

1. **Local colour, loose.** Five to eleven broad marks in the subject's real
   colours, multiplied together, roughly agreeing with where the object is.
2. **What is in it.** A liquid line across the form. One of the few marks in
   the study that has to be accurate: put it off level and the object tips over.
3. **The label**, and the lettering on it.
4. **The six**, going round the form and disagreeing with all of it.
5. **White**, last, over everything.

Reverse any two of those and the picture becomes an illustration.

## The broken contour

An outline in one colour is a colouring book. What these drawings do instead is
go round the form two or three times in different inks, in arcs with gaps
between them, each arc sitting a few pixels off the true edge. Three separate
things are happening and all three are load-bearing.

**It is broken.** A closed unbroken outline flattens whatever is inside it into
a sticker. Gaps let the fill and the paper meet, and the eye closes the shape
anyway — it is very good at that, and the drawing gets the credit.

**It is off-register.** Every arc is displaced along the normal, in or out at
random. A contour exactly on the edge reads as a *border*; a contour beside the
edge reads as somebody deciding where the edge was. (The crayon study arrived at
the same misregistration from a completely different direction, for its accent
colour. It seems to be a general property of drawings that look handmade.)

**Each arc is a different ink**, chosen with no regard for what it is going
round.

## Six, and not any six

Six inks picked freely come out with three yellows in them about a third of the
time, and a palette with three yellows in it has four colours — the strip in the
corner then promises a variety the drawing cannot deliver. So the draw is
rejection-sampled on hue spacing, and two of the six are held to be dark enough
to carry a contour on white. A set of six pastels cannot draw an outline, and
the outline is the drawing.

## A lathe and four attachments

Every object in the set is a **profile turned about a vertical axis** — a list
of half-widths from the lid to the foot — plus attachments. That is not a
shortcut, it is what the subject *is*: bottles, jugs, jars, vases, cups,
decanters, flasks and teapots are all objects made on a wheel or in a two-part
mould, and both of those are lathes. Get the lathe right and thirty-two
silhouettes cost thirty-two lines of numbers.

The attachments are where the family splits, and they carry more identity than
the profile does. A handle makes a jug. A spout and a lid make a teapot. A crown
cap and a waist make a Coke bottle and nothing else in the world.

## Smooth what grew, keep the corners on what was made

The single worst bug in this half, and the second time this repository has hit
it. Shapes are smoothed through their midpoints by default, which is what a hand
does and is right for a head, a leaf or a cheek. It is catastrophic for anything
manufactured: the first labels were rectangles and came out as **circles**, with
the word sitting in the middle of a lozenge.

The crayon study met exactly this when its doorframes turned into archways and
its stairs into a wobble. Reaching it again from a different medium is a good
sign that it is a rule rather than an incident: *smooth what grew, keep the
corners on what was made.*

## The lettering

A typeface set on a label would sink the whole picture instantly — you would see
a render with type composited onto it. Handwriting is the thing everybody is an
expert at reading, and it is the one mark that proves a hand was involved.

So the alphabet is polylines in a unit box, drawn with the same wobbling brush as
everything else, with each letter leaning and sitting slightly differently.
Uniform lettering is a font, and a font is exactly what this must not look like.

It is also **fitted to the band rather than set at a fixed size**. A word wider
than the label it is on reads as a mistake rather than as a hand — the one
failure in this style that cannot be passed off as looseness.

For the script logo there is a second function that draws the *rhythm* of a
signature — a big initial, a run of humps, a tail that goes back under the word —
and does not attempt the letters. Nobody reads a script logo, they recognise its
shape. Look closely at the scrawl on the reference bottle and there is no second
C in it at all.

## Faces

The objects are lathes and the faces are not, so they are a separate grammar —
but they keep the rule that matters. Skin is the only local colour on the page,
and even that is a flat unmodulated fill; hair is whichever of the six was going
spare, at full strength.

Three things carry the likeness, and none of them is detail.

**The silhouette of the hair.** More than the features, more than the head.
Cover the face of any of these and you can still tell them apart; cover the hair
and you cannot. The first pass built every cut out of overlapping ellipses and
they all came out as the same helmet, because an ellipse has no corners — and
the entire difference between a bob and long hair is *where the corners are*.
Ovals survive only for curls, where being a pile of ovals is the correct answer.

**The tilt.** A head that is exactly upright is a passport photograph.

**How little is in the middle.** Two marks for eyes, one for a nose, two for a
mouth. Every extra mark inside the oval costs expression rather than adding it,
which is the opposite of what it feels like while drawing.

Neck, then shoulders, then head, each covering the last — drawn the other way
round there is a white gap under the chin, and a head floating above a shape is
not a portrait of anybody. The shoulders are narrow and have a real corner in
them: wide and rounded, they came out as a dome with a head on top, which is the
shape of a chess piece.

## Nothing here is simulated

This is the third medium in the repository and the first one that is honestly a
*screen* medium. The crayon study deposits pigment a pixel at a time because a
wax stick on rough paper is a physical event. The watercolour study solves a
fluid because water is one. A brush in a drawing app is neither: it is a stamped
round nib dragged along a path at a flat colour and an alpha, and pretending
otherwise — adding grain, adding bleed — is what makes digital drawings look
like bad photographs of paintings.

So there is no paper here, no tooth, no water. A mark is a path with a round
cap. What has to be got right instead is everything about *where the marks go*,
because with no medium to hide behind that is all there is.

It is also, by a distance, the cheapest of the three. A drawing costs a
millisecond or two, against five for a crayon scene and a hundred and eighty for
a solved wash, and the whole gallery of two thousand builds in about fifteen
seconds on a machine with no GPU at all. That is the medium being honest about
itself: there is nothing to simulate, so there is nothing to pay for.
